# The Skills Section Most CVs Get Completely Wrong

A skills section should help employers identify relevant capabilities. A long list of generic qualities or every tool you have encountered makes that task harder.

Start with the target role and select skills you can demonstrate. The section is a concise index to your evidence, not a substitute for experience or projects.

## Separate capability from personality claims

Communication and teamwork can matter, but almost anyone can list them. Show those qualities through a relevant example, such as explaining reports to a nontechnical audience or coordinating a cross-team handover.

Use the skills section for clearer professional terms where appropriate: financial reporting, inventory reconciliation, SQL, campaign analysis or training coordination. Select only those that accurately describe your background.

## Group skills for the reader

An IT applicant might group languages, tools and methods. A finance applicant might separate accounting activities from systems. A general operations applicant may only need a short, well-ordered list.

Avoid overcomplicating the grouping. The reader should not need to understand your personal classification system before finding an essential skill.

## Describe proficiency honestly

Stars and percentages rarely explain ability. “Excel: pivot tables and reconciliation workbooks” is clearer than “Excel: 85%.” A student learning Python can label it as project experience instead of implying advanced professional use.

Do not list a tool merely because it appears in the vacancy. If you have used a related tool, name the real one and explain transferable work in the experience section.

## Connect skills to examples

Check your most important skills against the rest of the CV. Where did you use each one? What did you produce? Could you answer an interview question about it?

If a central skill appears nowhere else, either add accurate evidence or reconsider its prominence. A course can support learning, but course completion alone does not prove extensive application.

## Keep the list current

Remove obsolete or irrelevant tools when they distract from the target role. Add new skills after meaningful use, not simply after seeing a tutorial. Review the order for each application without changing the underlying facts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I remove all soft skills?

No. Demonstrate important interpersonal skills through specific work examples, and use the skills list selectively when a term is relevant.

### Is a longer skills section better?

Not necessarily. A focused, credible list is easier to assess than a large collection of weakly supported keywords.
